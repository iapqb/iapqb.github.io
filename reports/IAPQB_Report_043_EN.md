# Stuck in the bank's chatbot

**What financial regulators found when customers took problems to chatbots, and what they now expect**

IAPQB Report No. 43 · Practice report · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- In June 2023 the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) reported that all 10 of the largest US commercial banks used chatbots. Complaints described loops, missed disputes and no way to reach a person [1].
- The CFPB said chatbots must follow the same laws as the processes they replace. In 2025 it ordered Cash App's operator, whose automatic replies had stalled error reports, to provide live customer service around the clock [1, 2].
- The CFPB's list of research reports on advanced technology shows no later report on chatbots, and in August 2026 it stopped publishing consumers' complaint narratives [3, 4].
- Regulators and lawmakers in the US, UK, Hong Kong and the EU now require or praise four practices: complaints enter the formal process, customers can reach a person, AI use is disclosed, and named senior people answer for it [2, 5, 6, 7, 8].

## 1. A familiar situation

A complaints officer at a bank reviews chatbot transcripts. In one, a customer typed three times that a card payment was not hers. Each time, the chatbot showed her recent transactions and a help link. The chat was logged as a resolved enquiry, and nobody opened a dispute. The officer has to decide whether the bank had already received a complaint.

## 2. Why it happens

Financial firms introduced chatbots largely to cut the cost of human agents, the CFPB found [1]. Much of the industry used simple rule-based chatbots that offer menus or react to keywords. With chatbots and scripted staff, "only specific words or syntax" may trigger the recognition of a dispute, so their ability to recognise one may be limited [1]. Chatbots built on large language models can also give wrong answers that sound right; the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) calls this hallucination [1, 5].

Like the processes they replace, the CFPB wrote, chatbots "must comply with all applicable federal consumer financial laws" [1]. Under Regulation E, a US rule, a financial institution must investigate "any oral or written notice of error" about an electronic transfer that meets the rule's conditions. It must decide within 10 business days, or up to 45 days if, among other steps, it provisionally credits the account [9].

> **IAPQB view.** In the complaints the CFPB quoted and in the Cash App order, the harm came after customers had already asked for help: the same questions in a loop, template replies that closed cases, and no person to take the matter further. In both, the regulator measured the automated channel against duties firms already had [1, 2].

## 3. Three cases

### 3.1 United States, 2023: complaints about bank chatbots

On 6 June 2023 the CFPB published an issue spotlight, Chatbots in consumer finance. It cited an estimate that about 37% of the US population used a bank's chatbot in 2022 [1]. From complaints and a market review, it described "doom loops" of repeated answers with no way to reach a person, and chatbots that missed disputes, gave wrong information or crashed [1]. One credit card customer wrote that the virtual assistant "kept sending me in circles" on the day a payment was due [1].

The CFPB warned that "the technology may fail to recognize that a consumer is invoking their federal rights", and that firms may be liable [1]. Firms should avoid making a chatbot their main channel when it is "reasonably clear" that it cannot meet customers' needs, it said [10]. The report set no new rules. It said the CFPB would "hold entities that fail to meet their obligations to account" [1].

### 3.2 United States, 2025: automatic replies to fraud reports

On 16 January 2025 the CFPB ordered Block, which runs the payment app Cash App, to pay a $55 million penalty and up to $120 million in redress [2]. The order found that Cash App had no live customer support until February 2021; its phone number played a recording that sent people back to the app [2]. When consumers sent notices of error through the app, Block answered with automatic templates called "macros". One asked for information that Regulation E does not require, and without it the investigation stopped or never started. Block's "pushback" macros told customers that nothing more could be done and that the case would be closed [2].

The order requires live customer service 24 hours a day, with people answering by phone for at least 12 hours and by chat for at least 18 hours each day [2]. On 16 November 2026 the CFPB listed the case as "Post Order/Post Judgment" [11].

### 3.3 United States, 2026: complaint narratives no longer published

The 2023 spotlight is still online, and the CFPB's list of research reports on advanced technology shows no later report on chatbots [1, 3]. On 14 August 2026 the CFPB stopped publishing consumers' complaint narratives in its public database, calling them unverified and one-sided. The CFPB said it would keep collecting and responding to complaints [4].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**Complaints and error reports go into the formal process.** Under the Cash App order, live staff must be able to take, resolve or escalate complaints and notices of error [2]. The UK Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) says firms' complaint procedures must cover complaints about AI decisions, which customers can then take to the Financial Ombudsman Service free of charge [7].

**Some topics go straight to a person.** In a 2025 report on consumer support, the FCA found firms that automatically passed chatbot questions about bereavement to staff. One firm's web chat flagged words linked to vulnerability and moved those customers up the queue [6]. Its Consumer Duty guidance says firms that support customers mainly through one channel will likely need "a real-time human interface, such as a phone service", for problems such as fraud [12].

**Customers can ask for a human.** Since 19 June 2026, EU countries must apply rules on financial services sold at a distance. When a firm uses online tools such as chatbots, consumers can request and obtain human intervention before the contract and, in justified cases, after it [8]. The HKMA asks banks starting to use generative AI with customers to let them opt out and request human intervention on AI-made decisions as far as practicable [5].

**Customers are told it is AI, and what it cannot do.** The HKMA expects banks to disclose their use of generative AI and its limitations [5]. In Australia, only 10 of 23 financial firms reviewed by the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) had policies on telling consumers about AI use [13].

**Named senior people answer for the chatbot.** The HKMA says a bank's board and senior management "should remain accountable for all the GenAI-driven decisions and processes" [5]. The FCA says firms using AI "remain responsible" for meeting its rules, and in banks any use of AI in a business area falls within a named senior manager's responsibilities [7]. It plans no extra rules for AI [14].

**Outcomes are checked, as well as speed.** The FCA said firms should look beyond contact rates and wait times. Its good-practice examples include firms using tools such as speech analytics to check the quality of support [6].

## 5. What to take from this

**For staff who work with chatbots or read their transcripts.** In the Cash App case, messages sent through the app were notices of error, and template replies did not meet the duty to investigate [2]. A chat in which a customer disputes a payment can be a notice of error too [2, 9].

**For managers and compliance teams.** Regulators judged firms by what happened after customers asked for help [1, 2, 6]. These practices can be designed in: topics that always go to a person, a visible route to staff, and checks on outcomes beyond wait times [2, 6, 8, 12].

**For boards and senior managers.** A chatbot bought from a vendor is a third-party arrangement, and the FCA says firms are "responsible and accountable" for the regulatory duties that apply to such arrangements [6]. ASIC found that not all firms relying on outside AI models had arrangements to manage the risks [13].

## 6. Limits of this report

All three cases come from the United States. The complaints the CFPB spotlight quotes are consumers' own accounts, which the complaint process does not verify [1, 4]. The Cash App replies were fixed templates. Block neither admitted nor denied the findings [2]. The EU directive works through national laws, which this report did not check [8]. The UK senior managers regime is being reformed [15]. Pages of the Monetary Authority of Singapore could not be opened. This report does not test any chatbot.
