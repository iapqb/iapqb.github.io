# When AI helps teachers mark

**What England's education department and qualifications regulator allow, and what a government-commissioned test of an AI feedback tool found**

IAPQB Report No. 39 · Practice report · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- In England, Department for Education (DfE) guidance lets teachers use AI to help draft feedback. The teacher keeps the judgement, and pupils' work must not train AI models without permission or a copyright exception [1, 2].
- A trial tool built for the DfE found 92% of the errors in 47 test sentences, and missed 41% of the known errors in example pupil essays. A 2025 press release called the 92% "feedback accuracy" [3, 4].
- Ofqual, the qualifications regulator, says AI may not be the sole marker in regulated qualifications. Where AI helps mark students' work for qualifications, Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ) guidance in force from September 2026 requires a human to review all the work and decide the mark [5, 6].
- Practice: teacher review, tighter limits for higher stakes, no pupils' work in training data, school approval of tools, and evidence for claims. EU rules for high-risk AI in education apply from 2 December 2027 [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

## 1. A familiar situation

A secondary teacher has more than 100 essays to mark by Monday. An AI tool offers to mark them in seconds, and its first comments look sensible. The teacher cannot see which errors it missed, whether it would give the same mark twice, or where the essays go once uploaded.

## 2. Why authorities set limits

A government press release called marking "a huge drain on teacher time" [4]. In a DfE-commissioned survey of 1,211 teachers from January to May 2025, 15% used generative AI at least sometimes for written feedback, and 5% for marking [11].

A language model can give different answers to the same question, so marks can change between runs [2, 12]. In DfE-commissioned research with 12 teachers trying an AI feedback tool, one said they would re-read the essays anyway; others feared heavy workloads could lead them and their colleagues to over-trust the tool [13]. The DfE adds that marking "helps the teacher to understand their students better" [2].

> **IAPQB view.** The three bodies below each leave the decision with a person: the DfE with the teacher, JCQ with a human assessor, and Ofqual by ruling out AI as the sole marker [2, 5, 6]. The one government-commissioned test of an AI feedback tool here shows what that person must still catch: on example pupil essays, the tool missed 41% of the known errors [3].

## 3. Three cases

### 3.1 England, 2023 to 2026: what the education department allows

The DfE's guidance, first published on 29 March 2023 and last updated on 12 August 2025, sees "more immediate benefits and fewer risks from teacher-facing use" of generative AI. Staff must check AI content, and any final document "remains the responsibility of the professional who produced it" and of their organisation. Schools "must not allow or cause students' original work to be used to train generative AI models" without permission or a copyright exception [1].

DfE support materials updated in May 2026 say AI may help draft comments on common misconceptions, while judgement about each pupil's feedback "remains firmly with the teacher". AI used to mark work "may produce inconsistent feedback", and it "shouldn't be used to make summative judgements or predictions about student attainment or progress" [2]. The DfE's product safety standards for suppliers, renamed from "expectations" and expanded on 19 January 2026, cover tools "that automate the marking of student work" [8].

### 3.2 England, 2023 to 2025: a government-commissioned test of an AI feedback tool

For a DfE project, the company Faculty built a trial tool that checks Year 4 writing against the national curriculum and drafts feedback [3]. On 47 test sentences, each written by GPT with one known error, it found and correctly classified 67% of the errors once GPT was added, and 92% after further changes in January 2024. On 11 example pupil essays with inserted errors, the final version missed 41% of them. Six teachers rated the feedback for teachers highly useful in 13 of 18 ratings. The report does not represent DfE policy [3].

In August 2024 the government announced a £3 million "content store" of curriculum guidance, lesson plans and anonymised pupil assessments, linking the 67% and 92% figures to DfE tests published that day [14]. In January 2025 it gave £1 million to 16 developers for marking and feedback tools, due by April 2025. The press release said training tools on the content store "can increase feedback accuracy to 92%, up from 67%", and that the tools "retain teacher oversight of the feedback" [4].

### 3.3 England, 2024 to 2026: AI may not be the sole marker

Ofqual's policy paper of 16 July 2026, replacing an April 2024 version, states that "AI may not be used as a sole marker in any assessments that are part of regulated qualifications", and sees potential for AI to support lower-stakes and classroom marking [5]. Its working paper of January 2026 found that current evidence "does not support an overall or general case for the validity of AI marking" in high-stakes qualifications, and that agreement with human marks alone is not enough to assure validity [12].

For coursework and other internal assessments, JCQ guidance effective from September 2026 lets each school or college decide, after considering data privacy, whether teachers may use AI tools to help mark. Where it does, "an AI tool cannot be the sole marker". A human assessor must review all the work and decide the mark "regardless of the outcomes of an AI tool", and remains responsible for it [6].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**A teacher reviews the work and decides the mark.** JCQ requires this whenever AI helps mark work for qualifications [6]. The DfE keeps feedback judgement with the teacher [2], and described the tools it funded as keeping teacher oversight [4].

**Limits tighten as the stakes rise.** Ofqual grounds its approach in the high stakes of qualifications [5]. In the European Union, AI in education that may decide access to education or a person's career, such as exam scoring, is high-risk. Duties for such systems, including human oversight, apply from 2 December 2027 under the "AI Omnibus" amendments, in force since 27 July 2026 [10].

**Pupils' work and data stay out of training.** Staff should check with the data protection officer or IT lead before entering pupils' personal data, and make sure it is not used to further train the tool [7]. Suppliers may not train models on pupils' or teachers' work without the copyright owner's consent [8].

**The school approves the tools staff use.** The DfE tells staff to check that a tool is approved by their school, to say when they have used AI and to fact-check the results [7].

**Claims about tools need evidence.** The DfE says suppliers "should not exaggerate the impact or capabilities of their tools" and should back claims with evidence [8]. Its £24 million EdTech Testbed Programme, set out on 24 September 2026, will test products, including generative AI tools, in up to 100 rapid evaluations and up to 15 randomised controlled trials or other rigorous evaluations [9].

## 5. What to take from this

**For teachers who use AI to mark or give feedback.** Treat AI marks and comments as a draft and read the work, since the DfE warns that AI marking may be inconsistent [2]. Names and assessment data stay out of AI tools unless staff have been advised it is safe [2, 7].

**For school leaders.** Which tools staff may use, for which marking tasks, and whether pupils' work may be uploaded are school decisions that a written rule can record [1, 6, 7]. DfE-commissioned researchers suggested that time is saved only if teachers trust the tool's accuracy, and that a person still needs to check [13].

**For education authorities, exam boards and teacher trainers.** The 92% figure in the 2025 press release came from 47 sentences written by GPT [3, 4, 14]. In example pupil essays, the same tool missed 41% of the errors [3]. Ofqual's working paper sees a need for evidence specific to each qualification, its candidates and its types of question [12].

## 6. Limits of this report

Most sources come from England, and rules elsewhere differ. The EU point comes from the European Commission's summary page [10]. The DfE-commissioned test covered one trial tool, checking spelling, punctuation and grammar in Year 4 writing, mostly on synthetic data, in late 2023 and early 2024 [3]. The DfE's collection on technology in education, as shown on 16 November 2026, lists no evaluation of the 16 tools funded in 2025 [15]. Survey figures are self-reported [11]. This report does not test any AI marking tool.
