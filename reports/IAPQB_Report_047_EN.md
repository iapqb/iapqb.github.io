# Who wrote this article?

**How news publishers now check and label articles that AI helped write**

IAPQB Report No. 47 · Practice report · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- From November 2022, CNET published 77 finance explainers drafted with its own AI engine, at first with the AI disclosure visible only on hover. The Verge reported that 41 were corrected [1, 2, 3].
- In November 2023, Sports Illustrated deleted product reviews credited to invented author names, and its publisher blamed a contractor [4, 5].
- In May 2025, a bought-in section in the Chicago Sun-Times recommended 15 books, 10 of which did not exist. The paper's editorial team had not reviewed it [6, 7].
- Several newsrooms now treat AI output as unchecked, label it visibly, verify new writers and check bought-in content. Since 2 August 2026, EU law has required a label on AI-written text on matters of public interest, unless it had human review under someone's editorial responsibility [8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

## 1. A familiar situation

An editor at a regional newspaper is closing the Sunday edition. A partner company has sent a ready-made summer section with a reading list and quotes from experts. Checking every title and quoted expert would take a day nobody has planned for. The section will carry the paper's name.

## 2. Why it happens

A language model writes by predicting likely next words, and its errors read as confidently as its facts. NIST calls this confabulation: "confidently stated but erroneous or false content" [13]. A reading list, an expert's quote and a writer's biography are familiar shapes that a model can fill with a book, an expert or a writer that does not exist.

All three cases involve content made to earn revenue: finance explainers written to rank in Google searches and carry affiliate ads [3], reviews from an e-commerce contractor [5] and sections bought to meet revenue goals [7]. A byline says who wrote a text and who answers for it. Here it named a staff team, an invented person or nobody [2, 4, 14].

> **IAPQB view.** In each case a rule already existed. CNET said an editor fact-checked every story [15], Sports Illustrated's publisher said its contractor reported running anti-AI software on all content [5], and King Features banned AI-made content [6]. Errors and invented content still reached readers. CNET's editors missed errors, Sports Illustrated's editorial staff had no part in the contractor's articles, and the Sun-Times printed the section without review [1, 5, 6]. Each publication then corrected or removed the content [1, 4, 6].

## 3. Three cases

### 3.1 United States, 2023: finance explainers with errors and a hidden label

From November 2022, the CNET Money team published short finance explainers drafted with an AI engine designed in-house [1]. They ran under the byline "CNET Money Staff", and readers learned that automation was used only by hovering over it [2, 16]. After Futurism reported this in January 2023, CNET moved the disclosure into view, and its editor-in-chief wrote that every article "is reviewed, fact-checked and edited by an editor with topical expertise" [3, 15, 16].

Futurism then found that the compound-interest explainer said $10,000 at 3% would earn $10,300 in a year, when the interest is $300 [16]. CNET's audit found more stories needing correction and some sentences too close to other published text, and it paused the tool [1]. The Verge reported corrections to 41 of the 77 stories [3].

### 3.2 United States, 2023: product reviews by writers who did not exist

On 27 November 2023, Futurism reported that Sports Illustrated had published product reviews by authors who could not be found outside its website, with profile photos sold on a site of AI-generated headshots [4, 17]. When an invented author was removed, the reviews moved to another invented name without a note [5]. The same day, the magazine said it had deleted several articles [4].

The publisher, The Arena Group, said its contractor, AdVon Commerce, had assured it that humans wrote the reviews, that AdVon's writers had used pen names in some articles, and that it had ended the partnership [4, 5]. A person involved told Futurism that some articles were produced with AI [5]. In December 2023 the company fired its chief executive, and his successor's spokesperson said the firing was unrelated to the AI issue [18].

### 3.3 Chicago, 2025: a reading list of books nobody wrote

On Sunday 18 May 2025, the Chicago Sun-Times carried a special section licensed from King Features, a unit of Hearst [6]. Its reading list recommended 15 books by real authors, and 10 of the titles did not exist. The section's freelance writer blamed his use of AI and his failure to check the output [7]. Percival Everett, for example, never wrote The Rainmakers [14].

The section went into the paper without editorial review, and readers were not told that it came from a third party [6]. A newsroom review found multiple errors and unverifiable claims in each of the 10 stories that named sources, some with invented sources and quotes [7]. King Features said it banned AI-made content and dropped the writer, who said his contracts had no AI policy [6, 7].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**AI output is treated as unchecked source material.** The Associated Press standards of August 2023 say AI output "should be treated as unvetted source material" and that staff do not use ChatGPT to create publishable content [8]. Chicago Public Media, which owns the Sun-Times, forbids publishing AI-made content without human review [9].

**Review includes a fact-check, and a named party answers for it.** Since 2 August 2026, EU law has required disclosure of AI-written text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, unless it has had human review or editorial control and someone holds editorial responsibility [11; 12, para 153]. The Commission's guidelines call fact-checking "a minimum requirement", exclude "cursory editorial approval", and ask for whoever holds editorial responsibility to be named publicly [12, paras 134, 135, 138].

**Labels sit where readers will see them.** CNET moved its disclosure into view and gave the editor a shared byline [1, 15]. The EU guidelines ask for labels that people can perceive without "performing dedicated actions" [12, para 132]. Of 52 newsroom AI guidelines studied in 2023, 47 mentioned transparency, but only 8 said how to disclose [19].

**New contributors are verified.** In May 2025, WIRED retracted a freelance story that turned out to be an AI fabrication, although two AI-detection tools had rated it as likely human-written. WIRED said first-time contributors should generally get a fact-check and a senior edit, and that editors "should always have full confidence that writers are who they say they are" [10].

**Bought-in content meets the same standards and is labelled.** The Sun-Times said it would identify third-party licensed content and make it comply with its journalistic standards [6], and its owner later said it planned to stop buying special editorial sections from King Features [7]. The AP encourages journalists to make sure material from other sources is free of AI-generated content [8].

**Corrections are public and say what changed.** CNET added editors' notes explaining each correction [1], the Sun-Times published a newsroom review of the section [7], and WIRED replaced its story with an editor's note [10].

## 5. What to take from this

**For people who write or edit with AI at work.** Check every title, name, quote and figure in AI output against an original source before publication. The Sun-Times list put real authors' names on books that did not exist [7], and AI-detection tools rated WIRED's fabricated story as likely human-written [10].

**For editors and managers.** List each route by which ready-made content arrives, such as contractors, syndicated sections and new freelancers, and name the editor who checks it [6, 10, 12]. When content is bought to meet revenue goals, as at the Sun-Times, count the checking time in its cost [7].

**For journalism schools, trainers and employers.** Teach checking AI output and writing disclosures that readers notice; most of the 52 newsroom policies studied did not say how to disclose [19].

## 6. Limits of this report

All three cases involve US publications. Most facts come from news reports and the publishers' statements; the sources describe no court or regulator decision on these cases. Whether AI wrote the Sports Illustrated reviews is disputed [5, 18]. The figure of 41 corrected CNET stories comes from The Verge; CNET's note gives no number [1, 3]. The policy study was read as a 2023 preprint; the peer-reviewed 2025 version did not open, and its figures may differ [19]. The EU guidelines are non-binding [12, para 5], and the sources used here report no enforcement case under Article 50. This report does not test any AI tool.
