# Who decides when care ends

**What two lawsuits, a Senate inquiry and Medicare rules say about tools that predict how long patients need care**

IAPQB Report No. 44 · Case file · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- Medicare Advantage members, and the estates of members who died, have sued UnitedHealth and Humana. They allege that the insurers used a tool called nH Predict to deny coverage for care after hospital stays [1, 2]. UnitedHealth says the tool does not make coverage decisions [3, 4].
- In 2025 both courts allowed contract claims about the insurers' promises on who assesses each case. Both cases are still before trial [1, 2, 5, 6].
- A 2024 Senate staff report found that UnitedHealthcare's denial rate for such care rose from 10.9% in 2020 to 22.7% in 2022 as it worked to automate reviews. It also found evidence that, in some cases, insurers may press reviewers to follow predictive tools [7].
- Medicare rules require decisions based on the patient's own record, with clinician review of expected denials. A predicted stay cannot by itself end care [8, 9, 10].

## 1. A familiar situation

A care coordinator at a health insurer opens the file of a woman in a nursing home after hip surgery. The software predicts a 16-day stay, and today is day 15. Yesterday's therapy notes say she still cannot walk alone. His team's report compares actual stays with predicted ones. He must decide whether to start the notice that ends her coverage, or ask a physician to read the notes first.

## 2. Why it happens

Care after a hospital stay, for example in a skilled nursing facility, is called post-acute care [7, p. 14]. It needs approval before or during the stay, so people who continue after a denial often pay for it themselves, the Minnesota court noted [1]. Court filings and a Senate staff report say nH Predict projects a patient's care needs from similar patients. One naviHealth document appears to project 16.6 days, and the patient's lawyer said coverage was denied on day 17 [1; 7, p. 24].

A projection from similar patients describes a group, and one patient can need more care or less. The insurers told Senate investigators that their tools could only approve requests, and that other cases went to human reviewers [7, p. 51].

> **IAPQB view.** In each case below, the insurers say that any denial comes from a human reviewer [7, pp. 13, 51]. The open questions are how free those reviewers were to depart from a predicted number of days, and whether anyone looked at the patient again before care stopped. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) requires that second look [10].

## 3. Three cases

### 3.1 Minnesota, 2023: families sue UnitedHealth over nH Predict

On 14 November 2023, the estates of two deceased patients filed a class action in federal court in Minnesota against UnitedHealth Group, UnitedHealthcare and naviHealth, the UnitedHealth company behind nH Predict [1, 11; 7, pp. 13, 23]. The plaintiffs allege that UnitedHealth used the tool in place of physicians, that staff had to follow it under threat of dismissal, and that over 90% of claim denials are reversed on appeal [1]. The complaint alleges targets to keep skilled nursing facility stays within 1% of the projected days [11]. UnitedHealth denies that the tool makes coverage decisions. A naviHealth spokesperson told CBS News so, STAT reported that UnitedHealth called the algorithm a guidepost, and in court UnitedHealth calls the idea a "faulty premise" [3, 4, 12].

On 13 February 2025, Judge John R. Tunheim dismissed five of the seven claims as pre-empted by the Medicare Act, and let the two contract claims proceed: the plan documents said "Clinical Services Staff and Physicians make decisions" about care [1]. Discovery continues. The defendants' expert disclosures are due on 25 January 2027 [4, 5].

### 3.2 Kentucky, 2023: the same tool at a second insurer

On 12 December 2023, Humana members filed a similar class action in federal court in Kentucky [6]. They allege that Humana used nH Predict to make coverage decisions and disciplined staff who departed from it. One member alleges seven denials for the same care within 30 days, each after a successful appeal [2]. On 14 August 2025, Judge Rebecca Grady Jennings let four of the eight claims proceed, including breach of contract and a fraud claim over Humana's alleged failure to disclose its use of AI [2]. Both courts excused the members from first finishing Medicare's appeals process [1, 2]. The members asked for class certification on 5 November 2026, and Humana's response is due on 19 February 2027 [6].

### 3.3 Washington, 2024: Senate staff examine three insurers

On 17 October 2024, the majority staff of the US Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations reported on more than 280,000 pages of documents from UnitedHealthcare, Humana and CVS [7, p. 4]. UnitedHealthcare's denial rate for prior authorisation requests for post-acute care rose from 10.9% in 2020 to 16.3% in 2021 and 22.7% in 2022, as it worked to automate the process. For skilled nursing facilities, it rose from 1.4% in 2019 to 12.6% in 2022 [7, pp. 4, 24]. The staff found evidence that, in some cases, insurers "may be pressuring human reviewers to follow the recommendations of predictive technologies" [7, p. 51].

The report said the February 2024 CMS memo (section 4) did not explain how to keep a predicted stay from influencing the review, and recommended rules to prevent this [7, pp. 51-52]. CMS proposed guardrails for AI in December 2024, and in April 2025 decided not to finalise them [13, p. 15795]. Its April 2026 rule adds none [14].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**Each decision rests on the patient's own record.** Since January 2024, plans must base medical necessity decisions on several factors, including the enrollee's medical history, physician recommendations and clinical notes, a rule still in force [8, 10]. A tool may assist, CMS says, and one that decides "based on a larger data set instead of the individual patient's medical history" breaks the rule [10].

**The patient is re-assessed before care ends.** A predicted length of stay "alone cannot be used as the basis to terminate post-acute care services", CMS says. Whether care is still needed "can only be determined by re-assessing the individual patient's condition" before the notice is issued, and in a fast appeal the plan bears the burden of proof [10].

**A qualified clinician reviews each expected denial first.** A physician or other appropriate professional with relevant expertise must review any expected medical necessity denial before it is issued [9]. The American Medical Association's 2023 policy asks for human examination of patient records before a denial, and for reviewers with "no incentive to deny care" [15]. In the CMS WISeR model, run in Original Medicare in six states from January 2026, licensed clinicians determine all non-payment recommendations from companies using AI, which receive a share of the spending they avert [16].

**Oversight leaves records that courts can order produced.** The Minnesota court ordered UnitedHealth to produce its AI Review Board's documents on nH Predict and the names of staff involved in termination notices for 300 members of the proposed class. It did not order the tool's data or source code [4].

## 5. What to take from this

**For clinicians and reviewers who see a tool's projection.** CMS treats a predicted length of stay as an aid. Ending care needs a fresh assessment, and in a fast appeal the plan must give a specific and detailed explanation [10].

**For managers of review teams.** Both lawsuits allege discipline for staff who departed from the projections, and one alleges targets tied to them [2, 11]. Measuring reviewers on how closely stays match predictions works against the CMS rule; measuring whether they read current notes and recorded a patient-specific reason supports it [8, 10].

**For insurers' boards and compliance teams.** Two courts have let members sue on plan promises about who decides, and one has ordered an AI review board's records produced [1, 2, 4]. CMS has not adopted the rules on predictive tools that the Senate staff asked for, so each insurer decides how to keep a tool from steering its reviewers [7, pp. 51-52; 13, p. 15795; 14].

## 6. Limits of this report

Both lawsuits are before trial. The rulings took the plaintiffs' allegations as true, and no court has decided whether either insurer used nH Predict to deny care [1, 2, 4, 5, 6]. The Senate report is a majority staff report based on the insurers' documents, and it could not say how far Humana used automation to deny requests [7, pp. 4, 7]. Case status comes from published opinions and Georgetown Law's litigation tracker, without the full court dockets. State laws are not covered, and WISeR does not apply to Medicare Advantage [16]. No tool was tested.
