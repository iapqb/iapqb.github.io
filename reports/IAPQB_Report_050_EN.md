# When an AI note-taker joins the call

**What a court, a bar association, a privacy regulator and a university now say about consent, confidentiality and keeping recordings**

IAPQB Report No. 50 · Practice report · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- AI note-takers can join video calls, record everyone and keep the result on the provider's systems. A class action reported in August 2025 says Otter.ai's note-taker recorded people who had not agreed, and that Otter used their words to train its AI [1, 2].
- On 13 August 2026 a US federal judge let wiretap, biometric and other claims go ahead and dismissed some others. The ruling does not decide whether Otter broke the law [2].
- US federal law accepts the consent of one party. California and Washington require all parties' consent to record private conversations, and Massachusetts bans secret recording without it [2, 3, 4]. New York City's bar association tells lawyers to get consent before an AI tool records a call [5, 6].
- Current rules combine five measures: consent from everyone, a notice people see, no AI assistants in sensitive meetings, an end date for recordings, and a human check of summaries [5, 7, 8, 9, 10].

## 1. A familiar situation

A project manager joins a video call with a client and two colleagues. A bot called "Notetaker" joins too, because one colleague has linked an AI note-taker to her calendar. Nobody mentions it. The client speaks about a dispute with a supplier, and afterwards the bot emails a summary to everyone invited. The client never agreed to be recorded and does not know who now holds the recording.

## 2. Why it happens

Otter's help pages describe settings that make its note-taker join meetings and share notes automatically [11]. The user who switches it on is one of several people recorded. Otter's terms make that user "solely responsible for providing any notices to, and obtaining consent from, individuals" [12].

US federal law allows a recording when one party consents, unless the aim is a crime or a civil wrong [3]. California and Washington require the consent of all parties for confidential or private conversations [2]. In Massachusetts, secret recording without the prior authority of all parties is a crime carrying up to five years in prison [4].

Otter's privacy policy says it trains its AI "on de-identified audio recordings and on transcriptions", which it says may contain personal information [13]. The New York City Bar notes that clients may speak differently when recorded, and that a stored recording could become an opponent's evidence [5].

> **IAPQB view.** In each case below, the person who turns on the note-taker is one of several people it records, and Otter's terms leave consent to that person [12]. The court, the New York City Bar and Harvard each asked about the other people: whether they knew about the recording and agreed to it [2, 5, 6, 7].

## 3. Three cases

### 3.1 California, 2025 to 2026: Otter.ai in court

On 18 August 2025 Gizmodo reported a class-action lawsuit against Otter.ai, filed the previous Friday in federal court. The plaintiff, a California man, had joined a Zoom meeting where Otter's note-taker was running. Otter replied that users start recordings and are responsible for any permissions needed [1]. Another suit was referred to the same judge in September 2025 [14]. The suits were combined as In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation, with seven plaintiffs from California, Illinois and Washington. They allege that the note-taker joins as a "silent participant" and that Otter keeps conversations and voiceprints to train its models [2].

On 13 August 2026 Judge Eumi K. Lee let the federal and California wiretap claims, the Illinois biometric claims and several others go ahead. She dismissed the computer-fraud claims, the Washington claim and some privacy claims, with permission to amend [2]. She noted that under federal law Otter's defence of being a party does not cover recording for a "criminal or tortious" purpose. In her view, under California law a provider which keeps conversations for its own purposes, as alleged, can count as a third-party eavesdropper. The Illinois claims say Otter took voiceprints without written consent and had no published schedule for destroying them. Under Washington's consent rule, she wrote that even if the bot "appeared in the meeting as another participant", the court could not infer that a plaintiff knew he was being recorded. The order decides only whether the claims may continue [2].

### 3.2 New York, 2025 and 2026: lawyers told to ask first

New York City's bar association has issued two ethics opinions on AI tools that record and summarise calls. The first, of 22 December 2025, says "clients must be notified, and their consent obtained" whenever an AI system records their calls. It lists points to check about the tool: where data is stored, for how long, whether it is used for training, and whether it can be deleted [5]. The second, of 5 August 2026, covers calls with others, such as opposing lawyers. It asks for the consent of all other parties and says "the default practice should be not to use AI tools" on such calls without a good reason [6].

### 3.3 Cambridge, Massachusetts, 2026: one university's current rules

Harvard University Information Technology's guidelines, read on 16 November 2026, say AI meeting assistants should not be used in Harvard meetings, except approved tools with contractual protections and accessibility accommodations [7]. With an approved tool, the rule is: "Only begin recording once all participants have consented." If anyone objects, the assistant is not used, and participants are asked to switch assistants off in meetings involving information at Harvard's Level 3 or higher. Users should delete transcripts when no longer needed, unless a litigation hold requires keeping them [7].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**Everyone is asked first, and one objection is enough.** This is the Harvard rule, and the New York City Bar asks lawyers for the consent of all parties [5, 6, 7]. Australia's privacy regulator, the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC), calls seeking consent "a matter of best practice" for AI meeting minutes [8].

**The notice reaches every participant.** Microsoft Teams tells all participants when a recording starts, and warns that some regions may require everyone's permission [9]. Harvard asks users to announce a bot that does not announce itself [7]. The OAIC accepts a notice with a chance to object only if it is "clear and prominent" [8].

**Sensitive meetings run without AI note-takers.** Harvard asks participants to disable assistants for Level 3 or higher information, and asks outside hosts not to record sensitive discussions [7]. The New York City Bar's default for calls with non-clients is no AI recording [6].

**Recordings get an end date.** Microsoft Teams recordings and transcripts expire after 120 days by default, and the period can be cut to one day [10]. Copilot in Teams can run "Only during the meeting" without saving speech-to-text data, though by default it requires a saved transcript [15]. Harvard's guidelines say to delete transcripts no longer needed [7], and the OAIC says personal information recorded without need must be destroyed or de-identified [8].

**A person checks the summary before it is shared.** Harvard asks users to correct AI summaries, mark them as AI-made and possibly inaccurate, and share them only on a need-to-know basis [7]. The New York City Bar asks lawyers to review every recording, transcript or summary, whoever or whatever prepared it [5].

## 5. What to take from this

**For people who use AI at work.** A note-taker records everyone on the call, so the Harvard and New York rules ask each person before it starts [5, 6, 7]. The people suing Otter were participants who say they never agreed [2].

**For managers.** Three decisions recur in these cases: which assistants are approved, how long recordings are kept, and who checks summaries. Harvard has written rules for all three, and Teams has a setting for the second [7, 10].

**For schools, training providers and employers.** The New York City Bar counts knowing how to turn off recording as part of a lawyer's competence [6]. Staff who use these tools need the same skills: switching off default recording, removing an unwanted bot and deleting a recording made by mistake [5, 7].

## 6. Limits of this report

The Otter case is undecided, and its facts are allegations. The full court record could not be opened; the order of 13 August 2026 was the newest document on the US government's GovInfo service on 16 November 2026. Some news sites block AI tools, so the first filing rests on one news report and the court record. Legal examples are from the US and Australia only. Product settings, terms and the undated Harvard page may change. No company's internal note-taker policy could be opened, and no tool was tested.
