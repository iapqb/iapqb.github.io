# Proving AI literacy under the EU AI Act

**What supervisors advise organisations to keep on file, and what organisations say they record**

IAPQB Report No. 34 · Practice report · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- Since 27 July 2026, Article 4 of the EU AI Act asks providers and deployers of AI systems to "take measures to support" the AI literacy of their staff. It does not require them to guarantee any "specific level" for any person [1, 2].
- The European Commission says no certificate is needed, organisations can keep an internal record of trainings, and staff knowledge need not be measured [3].
- The Dutch and German supervisors advise recording AI systems, users and measures; the German one lists each measure's type, content, length and participants [4, 5, 6].
- In the Commission's repository, many of the 42 organisations count participants and a few test knowledge. In a 2025 Dutch call for input, 22% of respondents said they monitored AI literacy [5, 7, 8].

## 1. A familiar situation

The HR manager of a mid-sized logistics company is asked by the board whether the company meets the EU's AI literacy rule. Staff have used a chatbot for emails and translations for a year. Some joined a webinar, most signed the AI policy, and a training provider offers certificates. The manager must decide what to keep on file.

## 2. Why it is hard to prove

Article 4 sets a duty to act, with no result to reach. Providers and deployers "shall take measures to support the development of AI literacy" of their staff, taking into account knowledge, experience, education, training and the context of use. The duty "does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy of any individual" [1]. This wording came with the Digital Omnibus on AI, in force since 27 July 2026, whose recital 8 says that "stringent obligations to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy" would not suit all types of providers and deployers [2, 9].

So there is no required level to prove. An organisation can show what it did, for whom, and why. The Commission says national authorities supervise Article 4 from 2 August 2026, and that a sanction may be more likely where there is proof of "an incident due to lack of appropriate training and guidance" [3].

> **IAPQB view.** The Commission and both national supervisors start from the same three things: the AI systems in use, the people who use them, and the measures chosen for them [3, 4, 6]. Completion rates, which many repository entries report, show coverage [7]. The German supervisor also asks for choices that are "plausibel und nachvollziehbar", plausible and traceable [6].

## 3. Four cases

### 3.1 European Commission, 2025 and 2026: no certificate, an internal record

The Commission's questions and answers on AI literacy, online since mid-2025 [5], were last updated on 20 October 2026 [3]. They say: "There is no need for a certificate. Organisations can keep an internal record of trainings and/or other guiding initiatives." Article 4 "does not entail an obligation to measure the knowledge of AI of employees". Relying on instructions for use "might be ineffective" in many cases, and staff who use ChatGPT for advertising texts or translation should be told about risks such as hallucination [3]. In the Commission's own staff training, some courses use multiple-choice quizzes, and each completed course gives a digital certificate [10].

### 3.2 Netherlands, 2025 and 2026: a plan, a baseline and a file

The Dutch Data Protection Authority (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens, AP) coordinates the supervision of algorithms and AI. Its guidance of 30 January 2025 outlines a multi-year action plan: list AI systems and the people and roles involved, measure a baseline (for example with a staff survey), set goals by risk, carry out measures such as training, and evaluate. The plan needs board approval, a budget, assigned management responsibility and regular reporting [4]. Guidance of 23 October 2025 adds that to show AI literacy, organisations should keep internal documentation on their approach and progress, and need not measure each person's level or obtain certifications (IAPQB translation) [5]. In the AP's April 2025 call for input, 22% of respondents said they monitored AI literacy, 45% somewhat and 33% not [5]. On 9 October 2026, the AP had still not been designated as AI market supervisor [11].

### 3.3 Germany, 2025 and 2026: what goes in the file

In June 2025 Germany's Bundesnetzagentur, then doing preparatory work for the AI Act, published a non-binding paper on Article 4. It recommends documenting measures well: "In this way they can prove at any time that they meet the requirements of Article 4" (IAPQB translation). The record should give each measure's type, content and time scope, and the people who took part. A lack of AI literacy can be seen as a breach of the duty of care, especially where damage results. Formalised or standardised training, external certification and an AI officer are not required [6]. Its web page, updated for the Omnibus, repeats the documentation items and adds that no level must be guaranteed for any person [12].

### 3.4 European Union, 2024 to 2025: what 42 organisations say they record

The Commission's repository of AI literacy practices listed 42 entries on 16 November 2026, gathered in surveys in 2024 and 2025 [3, 7]. Entries must meet "minimum criteria of transparency and reliability", and copying one "does not automatically grant presumption of compliance with Article 4" [3]. Many entries count participants [7]. Hexagon reports that over 20,000 employees, about 80% of its workforce, had completed basic AI training by mid-2025 [13]. A few entries test knowledge. Alteryx staff must pass an assessment before using internal AI models [8], and Palantir is experimenting with a questionnaire to be given regularly, for example once a year [14]. VUCAMIX, a Romanian firm of one to five people, uses no formal indicators [15].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**They start from a list of AI systems and the people who use them.** The AP's first step is an inventory of AI systems with the people and roles involved [4]. Mural keeps "AI Use Case Cards" as an auditable record of the AI systems it develops [16].

**They record what was done, its content and length, and who took part.** These are the Bundesnetzagentur's items [6, 12]. The Spanish AIR Institute keeps training records in a central database that its HR and quality teams review [17].

**They tie training to roles and to system access.** Alteryx gives role-based courses, links its assessment to access to internal AI models, and requires more training before staff use APIs [8].

**Some measure what staff know, with no legal duty to do so.** The AP suggests a baseline survey and, as an example, a yearly staff survey [4]. The Danish Agency for Digital Government surveyed the use and knowledge of generative AI before designing its basic course [18].

**They name an owner and refresh the training.** In the AP's call for input, 70% of respondents had appointed a responsible person [19], though no AI officer is required [3, 12]. The Bundesnetzagentur names changes in AI systems, in their use or in technology as reasons to refresh [12].

## 5. What to take from this

**For people who use AI at work.** The Commission says staff who use ChatGPT for advertising texts or translation should be told about risks such as hallucination [3]. A record of each person's courses, with their content and length, is part of what the employer can show [6].

**For managers.** Keep one file per AI system: who uses it, what they received and for how long, and why that fits the risk [4, 6]. The Dutch supervisor adds an owner and regular progress reports [4].

**For HR teams, training providers and employers.** The Commission and both supervisors say no certificate is required [3, 5, 6]. A course should leave a record of its content, length and participants that the employer can file [6]. Copying a practice from the repository does not by itself show compliance [3].

## 6. Limits of this report

Supervisors in other Member States may advise differently, and the German paper is not binding [6]. The Dutch guidance predates the July 2026 amendment [4, 5]. No Irish guidance on Article 4 was found on the pages opened; Ireland's AI Office was set up on 2 August 2026 [20]. This report did not confirm whether Germany's implementing law is in force. Repository entries are self-reported [3], and the AP assumes that its respondents are often front-runners [5]. The Commission dates the Article 4 amendments to mid-July 2026; this report uses the EUR-Lex date, 27 July 2026 [2, 3]. On 20 October 2026, the AI Board's recommendations and the Commission's practical examples were still future steps [3]. No enforcement decision under Article 4 was found in the sources opened.
