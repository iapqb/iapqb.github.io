# Selling AI that is not there

**How US regulators acted on false AI claims from 2024 to 2026, and what regulators expect before a claim is published**

IAPQB Report No. 42 · Practice report · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- In March 2024, the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) settled charges against two investment advisers that claimed AI abilities they did not have [1].
- In September 2024, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) announced Operation AI Comply. One target, DoNotPay, sold a "robot lawyer" without testing whether it worked like a human lawyer, the FTC said [2, 3].
- In later FTC cases, an AI detector sold as "98 percent" accurate scored 53 percent in independent tests on general-purpose content, and an AI listening service used no voice data, the FTC said [4, 5].
- Regulators expect evidence on file before a claim is published, tests that match the claim, a new check each time it is used, and one review for every channel [6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

## 1. A familiar situation

A marketing manager at an investment app is writing the launch page for a new feature. The product team calls it "AI-driven" and sends an accuracy figure from a test on last year's data. The model that would use clients' own data is planned for next year. The page is due on Friday. Nobody has asked what the figure measured.

## 2. Why it happens

AI claims help sell products and raise money. When the SEC announced its 2024 cases, its chair said new technologies "can create buzz from investors as well as false claims", and called the practice "AI washing" [1]. Buyers often cannot check such claims.

The cases below show three gaps. A plan was described as if it were running: Delphia intended to use client data in its algorithms and never did [10]. A product built for one setting was sold for general use: the FTC says Workado's detector was trained only to classify academic content [4]. A claim outlived its evidence: a Global Predictions figure from a December 2021 analysis stayed public for about two years [9].

> **IAPQB view.** In each case below, a regulator compared the marketing with the product, and the firm, the regulator said, had no evidence that closed the gap. The rules applied were existing ones: the SEC applied anti-fraud, marketing and compliance rules [9, 10], and in 2024 the FTC's chair said there is "no AI exemption from the laws on the books" [2].

## 3. Four cases

### 3.1 Washington, 2024: two investment advisers and AI they did not have

From 2019 to 2023, Toronto-based Delphia (USA) Inc. said in SEC filings, a press release and on its website that it used AI and machine learning on client data in its investment process. The SEC found that it "had not developed the represented capabilities" [1, 10]. Delphia admitted this to SEC examiners in July 2021 and corrected its brochure, yet similar claims went on in emails, a social media post and a press release until August 2023 [10]. San Francisco-based Global Predictions Inc. advertised "[e]xpert AI-driven forecasts" that it did not have, and called itself the "first regulated AI financial advisor" without documents to back the claim [9]. In settlements announced on 18 March 2024, the firms neither admitted nor denied the findings and agreed to pay civil penalties of $225,000 and $175,000 respectively [1].

### 3.2 Washington, 2024 and 2025: a robot lawyer, untested against lawyers

On 25 September 2024, the FTC announced Operation AI Comply, five actions against operations that "use AI hype" or sell AI tools that can be used to deceive [2]. DoNotPay advertised "the world's first robot lawyer"; the FTC's complaint says it "did not test whether the Service's law-related features operated like a human lawyer" [3]. The final order of January 2025 requires DoNotPay to pay $193,000, to notify subscribers from 2021 to 2023, and to make no claim of lawyer-level work "unless it has sufficient evidence to back it up" [11]. In December 2025, citing the Trump Administration's AI Action Plan, the FTC set aside its 2024 order against Rytr, a writing tool accused of enabling false reviews, saying the complaint "failed to satisfy the legal requirements of the FTC Act" [12].

### 3.3 Washington, 2025: an AI detector built for one kind of text

Workado, formerly Content at Scale AI, promoted its AI Content Detector as "98 percent" accurate and said it was developed using a wide range of material, including blog posts and Wikipedia entries [4]. According to the FTC, the model was only trained or fine-tuned to classify academic content, and independent testing put its accuracy on general-purpose content at 53 percent [4]. The final order of 21 August 2025 bars claims about how well such a product detects AI content unless Workado holds "competent and reliable evidence" each time a claim is made [8].

### 3.4 Washington, 2026: an "Active Listening" service with no voice data

From 2023, Cox Media Group offered small businesses a service called Active Listening [5]. Its website said "Voice Data AI" would give a weekly list of qualified customers, and its sales presentations until mid-2024 said active listening "is analyzed via AI to detect pertinent conversations" [5]. According to the FTC, the service "did not collect or use voice data in any manner". It was "nothing more than consumer email list buying", with lists from data brokers resold at a significant markup and drawn from across the country [5]. In August 2026, the FTC made final its orders against Cox Media Group and two other firms, which must pay $930,000 in total for redress to Cox Media Group's customers [13, 14].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**Evidence is on file before the claim is published.** The UK's non-broadcast advertising code says that "[b]efore distributing or submitting a marketing communication for publication, marketers must hold documentary evidence" for objective claims [6]. The SEC's marketing rule bars an adviser from advertising a material fact without a "reasonable basis for believing it will be able to substantiate" it when the SEC asks [7].

**The test matches the claim.** Under the Workado order, competent and reliable evidence means tests, studies or similar work, based on expertise in the relevant field, "conducted and evaluated in an objective manner by qualified persons" [8]. UK guidance from August 2026 gives an example: an AI diet assistant that claims to work out a meal's nutrition from a photo needs evidence of that accuracy, or the ad could be found misleading [15].

**The evidence is checked each time the claim is used.** The Workado order requires the evidence "at the time such representation is first made, and each time such representation is made thereafter" [8]. The Cox Media Group order requires the company to keep, for five years after a claim was last used, "all materials that were relied upon in making the representation" and any evidence that contradicts or qualifies it [14].

**One review covers every channel.** Global Predictions' compliance manual required written approval of all marketing before release and a log of approvals; the SEC found the firm did not follow it [9]. Delphia had no clear review roles and no social media policy, and after correcting its brochure it kept making the claim elsewhere [10].

**Disclosures describe what the system does now.** For automated investment tools, the SEC's examination priorities for fiscal year 2027 say examiners will generally assess whether "representations are fair and accurate" and whether "operations and controls are consistent with disclosures made to investors" [16].

## 5. What to take from this

**For people who write about AI products.** Ask for the test behind each AI claim, and check that it matches the task, data and users the claim describes [4, 8]. Label planned features as planned; Delphia's marketing presented a plan as a working service [10].

**For managers and compliance staff.** Keep the evidence with the approval record, check it again when a claim is reused or the product changes, and include emails and social media in the review [8, 9, 10, 14].

**For buyers and investors.** Ask what the AI does, on what data, and how its accuracy was measured. Cox Media Group advertised targeting based on conversations and, the FTC says, resold email lists [5].

## 6. Limits of this report

All four cases come from two US regulators and ended in settlements; the facts here are the regulators' account, which the SEC respondents and Cox Media Group neither admitted nor denied [1, 14]. The UK material is a code rule and guidance; no UK ruling on a false AI claim was found in the pages opened for this report. US policy and wording have also changed: the FTC set aside one order from its 2024 sweep [2, 12], and the SEC's 2027 examination priorities call these technologies "super intelligence (SI)" [16].
