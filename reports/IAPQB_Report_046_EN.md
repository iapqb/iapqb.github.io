# The debts a formula made

**What courts, a royal commission and an anti-corruption body found about who answers for Australia's Robodebt scheme**

IAPQB Report No. 46 · Case file · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

## In one minute

- From July 2015 to November 2019, an Australian Government system raised welfare debts by spreading tax office income data evenly across fortnights. The Commonwealth later admitted it had no proper legal basis for them [1].
- About 433,000 people were asserted to owe at least $1.763 billion. Court-approved settlements followed in 2021 ($112 million) and 2026 (up to $548.5 million) [1, 2].
- In July 2023 a royal commission called the scheme "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal". In March 2026 its two recommendations on automated decision-making were still "Ongoing" [3, 4].
- In March 2026 the National Anti-Corruption Commission found two people had engaged in serious corrupt conduct by misleading others about the scheme. Neither was referred for prosecution [5, 6].

## 1. A familiar situation

A service officer takes a call about a debt letter. Years ago the caller had a few months of casual work and a benefit the rest of the year. The system has spread her yearly income evenly across every fortnight and found an overpayment. To dispute it she needs old payslips. In 2017 the Ombudsman called such demands "not reasonable or fair" where people must collect evidence from several years ago [7, para 3.23].

## 2. Why it happens

In late 2014, lawyers at the Department of Social Services (DSS) advised that the law requires "actual fortnightly earning or receipt of income" to be considered, and that averaging might not be consistent with it [2, para 77]. Robodebt spread income data from the Australian Taxation Office evenly across fortnights [1, para 2]. Many welfare recipients do not earn a stable income, so averaging is unlikely to match their actual fortnightly income [1, para 5].

The Department of Human Services (DHS) estimated about 783,000 interventions in 2016-17, against about 20,000 a year under the manual process, and the system put "greater emphasis on the customer's responsibility" to accept the debt or provide information [7, paras 2.4, 3.22]. About 29 Administrative Appeals Tribunal decisions up to 30 May 2017 rejected averaging as a basis for debts [1, para 10]. The Royal Commission found that by early 2017 the scheme's "unfairness, probable illegality and cruelty became apparent" [3, p. 3].

> **IAPQB view.** One averaging rule produced the debts that the Commonwealth later admitted had no proper legal basis [1]. Both findings of serious corrupt conduct concern what officials told others about the scheme, in 2015 and 2017 [5, 6]. The legal advice and tribunal decisions came years before the scheme ended in November 2019 [1, 2].

## 3. What happened

### 3.1 Australia, 2016 to 2017: the system goes online

In July 2016 DHS launched a new online compliance intervention system. In April 2017 the Acting Commonwealth Ombudsman found its debts "accurate, based on the information which is available to DHS", and letters sent before 20 January 2017 "unclear and deficient in many respects" [7, summary; para 3.15].

### 3.2 Australia, 2019 to 2021: the Commonwealth concedes

On 27 November 2019, in a Federal Court test case, the Commonwealth consented to declarations that a debt raised from averaged tax data was invalid [1, para 10]. In the class action it admitted having no proper legal basis for such debts. It had recovered about $751 million and by the settlement had refunded $707.9 million [1, paras 4, 21]. On 11 June 2021 Justice Murphy approved a settlement, without admission of liability, under which it would pay $112 million inclusive of legal costs [1, para 8]. He said the case exposed "a shameful chapter in the administration of the Commonwealth social security system" [1, para 5]. He added that little in the material showed those responsible actually knew the system was unlawful [1, para 6].

### 3.3 Australia, 2023: the royal commission reports

The Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme reported on 7 July 2023 [2, para 24]. It wrote: "Robodebt was a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal, and it made many people feel like criminals" [3, p. 3]. As the Federal Court later recorded, it found that the scheme went ahead "without the legislative change to support averaging which DSS had said was needed", that Mr Morrison, as minister, "allowed Cabinet to be misled", and that an Ombudsman's report was procured by deception [2, paras 77, 80, 84]. The government accepted or accepted in principle all 56 recommendations [3, p. 8]. Six people were referred to the National Anti-Corruption Commission (NACC) [5].

### 3.4 Australia, 2024 to 2026: an anti-corruption inquiry and a second settlement

The NACC first decided not to investigate, the ABC reported in June 2024 [8]. Its Inspector found that the Commissioner, having declared a perceived conflict with one of the six, should have kept out of the decision entirely; an independent person was to reconsider it [9, 10]. An investigation began on 4 April 2025 [5].

In March 2026 the NACC found that one person had intentionally misled DSS officers preparing a 2015 submission to a Cabinet committee, and another had intentionally misled the Ombudsman in 2017. Both had engaged in serious corrupt conduct; the other four had not engaged in corrupt conduct [5, 6]. Neither was referred for prosecution. The NACC found "insufficient admissible evidence", and key admissions made to it "are not admissible in criminal proceedings" [5].

On 23 June 2026 Justice Beach set aside the 2021 settlement orders, let the applicants add a claim of misfeasance in public office (officials knowingly or recklessly acting unlawfully), and approved a new settlement of up to $548.5 million, including $475 million in compensation [2, orders 1-3; paras 13, 34, 65, 69]. The claim was not tried [2, para 72]. The judge called the scheme "a fiasco in public administration and an abrogation of ministerial responsibility" [2, para 1]. Fixed payments were expected from about October 2026 [11].

## 4. How practitioners handle it now

**Legal issues reach Cabinet before launch.** The government's March 2026 update marks as implemented rules that Cabinet advice should say whether a proposal needs a change in the law, and that Cabinet be "clearly informed of identifiable legal issues" [4, recs 15.1, 15.2].

**Legal advice is finished and recorded.** The government agreed that "legal advice cannot be disregarded because it is in draft form" [3, recs 19.5, 19.6]. At Services Australia, where finishing advice is not practicable, "Legal Officers must place a file note on the relevant file confirming the reason" [4, rec 19.6].

**Privacy policies must soon say where computers decide.** From 10 December 2026, privacy policies must list the kinds of decisions made with "a computer program" that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect a person's rights or interests, where the program uses their personal information [12]. Recommendation 17.1 also asks for "a clear path for those affected by decisions to seek review", plain-language explanations, and business rules and algorithms "made available, to enable independent expert scrutiny" [3, rec 17.1].

**The Ombudsman checks; an auditor may follow.** Recommendation 17.2 asks the Commonwealth to consider a body to "monitor and audit" automated decisions for their technical aspects and for "fairness, the avoiding of bias, and client usability" [3, rec 17.2]. In March 2026 both 17.1 and 17.2 were still "Ongoing" and needed legislation [4]. A duty to assist the Ombudsman is marked implemented [4, rec 21.1].

**Agencies name people accountable for AI.** Under the government's AI policy, updated from 15 December 2025, agencies must "assign an accountable owner" for each in-scope AI use case, with new requirements taking effect between 15 June and December 2026 [13].

## 5. What to take from this

**For people who work with automated decisions.** In Robodebt the warnings were in writing early: legal advice, tribunal decisions and the Ombudsman's report [1, 2, 7]. Both corruption findings concerned what officials told others [5, 6].

**For managers and senior officials.** The Ombudsman found the debts accurate on the data DHS held [7]; the Commonwealth later admitted they lacked a proper legal basis [1]. The reforms marked implemented put the legal check in writing [4].

**For auditors, reviewers and trainers.** The four points in Recommendation 17.2 can guide a review now; the body it proposed was still under consideration in March 2026 [3, 4].

## 6. Limits of this report

The Royal Commission's report and the NACC's full report could not be opened. The Australian Public Service Commission's code of conduct inquiries are not covered. Counts of people affected differ: about 433,000 in the Federal Court [1, 2], about 443,000 in an ABC report [6]. "Implemented" is the government's own label [4]. No later change to the Privacy Act start date was found [14].
