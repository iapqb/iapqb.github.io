Authorised to operate

What forklift rules in three countries teach about checking AI skills at work

IAPQB Report No. 30 · Perspective report · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

In one minute

  • In the United States, a worker may drive a forklift alone only after training and a workplace evaluation, and the evaluation is repeated at least every three years [1].
  • Great Britain's code and guidance ask for practical and theory tests, supervised work on the job and written authorisation [2]. South Korea's construction machinery law requires operators of forklifts that can lift 3 tonnes or more to hold a national qualification with a practical exam. Below that, a 12-hour course can replace the qualification [3, 4, 5, 6].
  • Since July 2026, the EU AI Act asks organisations to support AI literacy, with no set level. The European Commission says no certificate or measurement of staff knowledge is needed [7, 8, 9].
  • Under the US and British forklift rules, someone watches real work, the decision is recorded, and a near miss can lead to retraining. The AI rule leaves such steps to each organisation [1, 2, 7, 9].

1. A familiar situation

On her first day at a distribution centre, a new stock planner has two kinds of training. The forklift course ends with a supervisor watching her move pallets and signing her card. Her account for the company's AI assistant opens after a 20-minute online module with a quiz. Nobody watches her use it.

2. Why the rules differ

All three forklift systems include some theory about the machine [1, 2, 5, 6]. In all three, the decision about who may drive rests on observed driving: an evaluation in the workplace, a practical test, or supervised hours at the controls [1, 2, 5, 6].

OSHA, the US workplace safety regulator, wrote in 1998 that "evaluation is an essential element of any training program" [10]. The EU's 2026 amendment says data indicates that "stringent obligations to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy" create "an additional compliance burden, particularly for smaller enterprises" [8, recital 8].

IAPQB view. The forklift rules decide who may drive only after someone has watched the person drive, and they record that decision [1, 2, 4, 5, 6]. The amended EU rule leaves any check of AI skill to each organisation [7, 9]. Employers are therefore free to use the forklift method for AI work that others rely on.

3. Four rules

3.1 United States, 1998: from "trained" to "competent"

OSHA's old training rule began: "Only trained and authorized operators shall be permitted to operate a powered industrial truck." A final rule of 1 December 1998, in force from 1 March 1999, replaced it [10]. Employers must now ensure that each operator is competent "as demonstrated by" training and an "evaluation of the operator's performance in the workplace", run by people able to judge competence [1]. Refresher training is required after observed unsafe driving, an accident or near miss, a poor evaluation, a move to another type of truck or a workplace change that could affect safety. Performance is evaluated at least every three years, and the employer's certificate gives the operator's name, the dates and who trained or evaluated them [1]. OSHA expected the rule to prevent 11 deaths and 9,422 injuries a year [10].

3.2 Great Britain, 2013: tests, supervision and written authorisation

The Health and Safety Executive's code L117 is in its third edition (2013), with the code text unchanged since 1999 [2]. Employers should not let anyone operate a lift truck, "even on a very occasional basis", without basic training and testing, and trainees must pass "a test or tests, practical and theoretical" [2, paras 32, 46]. L117's guidance says specific job training covers the truck, site rules and working conditions. Familiarisation follows "on the job, under close supervision" [2, paras 56, 65]. The employer should then give written authorisation naming the operator, date, trucks and any limits [2, para 67]. Retesting or refresher training may follow an accident, a near miss or a change of working practices or environment. No fixed interval is set, and three to five years is given as an example [2, paras 78, 79].

3.3 South Korea, 2021: a qualification or a course for nearly every forklift

Under the Construction Machinery Management Act, which covers tyred forklifts with a driver's seat [11], an operator licence requires a national technical qualification and an aptitude test. For small machines, a course at a designated training body can replace the qualification [3, Art. 26]. Forklifts with a lifting capacity under 3 tonnes count as small machines [4, Art. 73; 5]. The national forklift qualification includes a practical exam in forklift work and road driving [6]. The small-forklift course has six hours of theory and six at the controls, with one instructor per machine and no final test [5].

Since 16 July 2021, labour rules have required the same qualification or course for electric forklifts with solid tyres used only away from roads, which the Act does not cover [11, 12]. Licence holders take a four-hour safety course every three years. It may be taught online and includes no driving check [4, Art. 83; 13].

3.4 European Union, 2026: support for learning, no required level

Article 4 of the AI Act has applied since 2 February 2025 [9]. The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on 27 July 2026 [8]. Article 4 now requires providers and deployers to "take measures to support the development of AI literacy" of their staff, without requiring them "to guarantee any specific level" [7].

The Commission's questions and answers, updated on 20 October 2026, state: "There is no need for a certificate." Organisations "can keep an internal record of trainings", and Article 4 "does not entail an obligation to measure the knowledge of AI of employees" [9]. Supervision and enforcement by national market surveillance authorities apply from 2 August 2026 [9]. For high-risk systems, deployers must assign human oversight to people with "the necessary competence, training and authority" [7, Art. 26(2)], from 2 December 2027 or 2 August 2028 depending on the system [7, Art. 113; 14]. The article does not say how competence is to be shown.

4. How practitioners handle it now

Training uses the real machine and the real site. OSHA requires training on the site's own conditions, and the British guidance calls specific job training and familiarisation essential [1, 2]. Article 4 asks organisations to consider "the context the AI systems are to be used in" [7], and the Commission says instructions of use alone are not sufficient [9].

A qualified person watches the work. OSHA requires a workplace evaluation by someone able to judge competence, and British instructors should be trained in assessment [1, 2]. In Korean practice sessions, each machine has its own instructor [5]. Article 4 contains no duty to measure staff knowledge [9].

The employer is answerable for who operates. US employers must certify each operator, and British employers should give written authorisation [1, 2]. Korean employers must not knowingly let unlicensed people operate [3, Art. 27-2]. For AI, the Commission accepts an internal record of trainings [9].

A near miss or a new machine leads back to training. The US rule and the British texts both treat a near miss and a move to another type of truck as reasons for more training [1, 2, paras 44, 78]. Korea adds a safety course every three years [4]. Article 4 names no triggers [7].

5. What to take from this

For people who use AI at work. Under the US and British forklift rules, a near miss is a reason for refresher training [1, 2]. A wrong AI output caught before it reached a client is a similar event, and reporting it lets a manager decide on more training.

For managers. Article 4 leaves the measures to the organisation [7, 9]. Applied to AI, the forklift method means observing real tasks with the tool, recording who observed and when, and checking again after an error or a change of tool [1, 2].

For schools, training providers and employers. In all three forklift systems, theory is one part, and driving watched by an instructor or examiner is required [1, 2, 5, 6]. An AI course that ends with a quiz covers the theory part only.

6. Limits of this report

This report compares rules. It does not show that observed checks reduce AI errors, and OSHA's figures on deaths and injuries prevented were a forecast made in 1998 [10]. A forklift task has a visible result and many AI tasks do not, so practical checks for AI are harder to design. Some British points come from L117's guidance, which carries less legal weight than the code [2]. Korean texts were translated by IAPQB.
