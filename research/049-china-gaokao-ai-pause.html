Chatbot photo features paused for China's national exam

How chatbot companies and exam authorities in China and England responded to AI and smart devices at high-stakes exams

IAPQB Report No. 49 · Case file · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

In one minute

  • In June 2025, four Chinese chatbots suspended or limited photo recognition during China's national college entrance exam, the gaokao. Asked why, those that suspended it cited fairness [1, 2].
  • Chinese exam authorities used gates that detect phones and smart glasses, radio signal blocking, repeated identity checks, and AI cameras whose alerts a person checks [3, 4, 5, 6].
  • In 2026, China's education ministry warned that carrying a phone, smartwatch or smart glasses into the exam room is cheating even if unused, and several provinces announced glasses checks at the exam-room door [7, 8, 9].
  • In England, phones and other communication devices were involved in 44.3% of student malpractice cases in summer 2025, and an exam-room poster in force from September 2026 lists smart glasses as banned [10, 11].

1. A familiar situation

An exam officer at a professional body is setting up a room for a certification test. Candidates leave their phones in a box at the door. One candidate wears thick-framed glasses. The rules sheet lists phones and watches and says nothing about glasses. The officer must decide whether to ask the candidate to remove them, and on what grounds.

2. Why it happens

A chatbot that reads images can turn a photo of an exam question into an answer. Cheating this way needs a device in the exam room, a signal out of it, and a service that answers. Exam authorities control the first two, with security gates, device checks and signal blocking [3]. The answering service belongs to private companies, and in 2025 some of them paused the photo feature [1, 2].

In 2026, Guangdong police said they were targeting cheating with smart glasses and smartwatches connected to AI models [12]. England's chief exam regulator said smart spectacles showing text that only the student can see might be next [13].

IAPQB view. In these cases, the rules that bound every candidate came from exam authorities: device checks, signal blocking, and a rule that carrying a connected device is cheating [3, 7]. The chatbot pause differed from app to app, was limited to exam hours, and was not publicly announced, as far as reporters found [1, 2]. By 2026, banned-item lists in China and England both named smart glasses [7, 11].

3. Four cases

3.1 China, 2025: chatbots stop reading exam photos

The 2025 gaokao had 13.35 million registered candidates [3]. Bloomberg reported on 9 June that apps including Tencent's Yuanbao and Moonshot's Kimi had suspended photo recognition during exam hours. Asked to explain, these chatbots cited the need "to ensure the fairness of the college entrance examinations". Alibaba's Qwen and ByteDance's Doubao still offered photo recognition, but refused questions about a photo of a test paper. Qwen said the service was temporarily frozen during exam hours from 7 to 10 June, and Doubao called the picture "not in compliance with rules" [1]. Gizmodo said it did not appear that any of the companies had publicly announced the restrictions [2].

3.2 China, 2025: gates, signal blocking and AI cameras

On 28 May 2025, the ministry and other departments called for upgraded smart security gates, focused checks for phones, smartwatches and smart glasses, full radio signal blocking inside exam sites, and intelligent patrols [3].

China Daily reported that in Liaoning, candidates at every test site had to pass two gates that detect phones, smart glasses and hidden earpieces, with handheld metal detectors after any alarm. More generally, it reported repeated identity checks by face, fingerprint or iris scan, and national radio authorities watching exam sites for suspicious signals [4]. Jiangxi said it would use AI patrol in every exam room and review the video province-wide after the exam [5]. Fujian used real-time AI patrol for the first time and said a person would investigate anything the system flagged [6].

3.3 China, 2026: glasses checked at the door

For 2026, with 12.9 million candidates registered, the ministry asked local authorities to raise the detection rate for phones and smart glasses [14]. Its warning of 2 June said that carrying a phone, smartwatch, smart glasses or other device that can send or receive information into the room is cheating whatever the reason, used or not, and voids all the candidate's results in that exam [7].

In Hubei, every candidate with glasses had to take them off under video monitoring for an invigilator to check [8]. Inner Mongolia told candidates who normally wear smart glasses to bring ordinary glasses, and said exam sites would not accept requests to wear them instead [9]. Guangdong police announced 28 measures covering all 608 exam sites in the province, including detection of unusual signals near them [12].

3.4 England, 2025 to 2026: devices top the malpractice count

Ofqual, England's exam regulator, counted 5,025 proven cases of student malpractice in the summer 2025 GCSE, AS and A level exams. Phones and other communication devices were involved in 2,225 of them (44.3%), the most common offence [10]. In June 2026, amid concerns about invisible earpieces and smart glasses advertised on social media, Chief Regulator Sir Ian Bauckham said: "We shouldn't underestimate the challenge involved here" [13]. A poster of the Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ), in force from 1 September 2026, lists smart glasses with phones, watches and storage or web-enabled devices [11]. JCQ's warning poster bans possession of a phone or other unauthorised material even without intent to use it, and warns of a penalty and possible disqualification [15].

4. How practitioners handle it now

Some providers pause the feature that reads the question. In 2025, four Chinese chatbots suspended or limited photo recognition during exam hours, each in its own way [1, 2].

Carrying a connected device is the offence. In China, carrying a device that can send or receive information voids all of the candidate's results in that exam, used or not [7]. JCQ bans possession of a phone even without intent to use it [15].

Entry checks and signal controls cover the whole site. Chinese exam sites combine smart gates, handheld metal detectors, repeated identity checks and radio signal blocking [3, 4, 8, 14].

AI cameras flag behaviour, and people decide. Hubei's system flags whispering, looking around, copying and carrying or using prohibited items, and alerts a video invigilator; penalties follow once the exam site confirms the case [8]. Jiangxi reviews the video after the exam, and Fujian said a person would investigate any alert [5, 6].

Written rules cover AI use in tests. Guidance from a committee of China's education ministry, reported on 12 May 2025, bans school students from directly copying AI-generated content as homework or exam answers, and from using generative AI to cheat [16].

Enforcement reaches the sellers of cheating equipment. China's 2026 plan includes campaigns against such sales, and police and education authorities warned that selling or using wireless cheating devices can lead to criminal charges [14, 17].

5. What to take from this

For people who sit tests at work or for a qualification. In China and England, the 2026 rules name smart glasses and watches, and carrying them into the room is the offence [7, 11, 15]. Several Chinese exam authorities told candidates who normally wear smart glasses to bring ordinary ones [9].

For AI providers. In 2025, the Chinese chatbots showed that one feature can be paused for a fixed exam window, with a stated reason [1]. The 2025 reports also show the limits: the apps did different things, no announcement was found, and no source measured whether the pause reduced cheating [1, 2].

For exam bodies, schools and training providers. Several Chinese provinces check glasses at the door, and Hubei and Fujian put a person between an AI alert and a penalty [6, 8, 9].

6. Limits of this report

The chatbot pause is known from news reports. Bloomberg's opening says Qwen disabled picture recognition; this report follows its later passage saying Qwen still offered it. DeepSeek is left out because Gizmodo's claim about it cites the Guardian, which could not be opened. No public company statement on the 2025 pause was found. Before the 2026 exam, several platforms told reporters that photo question answering and similar functions would again be restricted during exam hours [18, 19]. China's national radio quoted unnamed insiders as saying this followed regulators' requirements, which no official source here confirms [18]. The Chinese sources describe plans and give no figures on candidates caught with AI devices or on false AI alerts. Ofqual counts phones and other communication devices together. Chinese sources were translated for this report. No device or AI tool was tested.
