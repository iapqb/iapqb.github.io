The EU AI Act timeline after the 2026 changes

Which duties apply when, and which official text sets each date

IAPQB Report No. 33 · Rule explainer · Published 24 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

In one minute

  • Article 113 of the AI Act sets its start dates: 2 February 2025 for the bans and the AI literacy duty, 2 August 2025 for general-purpose AI models, and 2 August 2026 for most other rules, including transparency [1, 2].
  • The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026, moved the high-risk rules to 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028, simplified the literacy duty and added two bans from 2 December 2026 [2, 3, 4].
  • Enforcement began on 2 August 2026. By 14 October the Commission had taken its first enforcement actions, and its AI Office had sent requests for information [5, 6].
  • Practitioners take each date from the article that sets it and record each system's role, type and market date [7, 8, 9].

1. A familiar situation

A compliance officer at a logistics company has a slide from 2024 that says the EU AI Act applies from 2 August 2026. The company uses a customer chatbot, an image generator and AI route-planning software. In November 2026 the board asks which rules apply. The slide gives one date, and the amended law gives several.

2. Why the dates are hard to pin down

The AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 [10]. Article 113 set four start dates, from 2 February 2025 to 2 August 2027 [1], and the Omnibus moved some and added others [3, Art. 1(40)].

Three facts decide which date applies: the organisation's role (a provider places a system on the market, a deployer uses it) [1, Art. 3], the type of system, and when it first reached the market, since Article 111 treats older systems differently [7]. Enforcement can start later than the duty: the bans and the AI literacy duty applied in February 2025, but the AI Act Service Desk dates their enforcement from 2 August 2026 [5].

IAPQB view. In the four cases below, every start date for providers and deployers sits in Article 111 or Article 113, as amended in July 2026. On 16 November 2026, two Commission pages gave older or different dates [11, 12]. A plan built on such summaries could carry a wrong deadline.

3. Four cases

3.1 European Union, 2025: the first bans and the literacy duty apply

Chapters I and II, which hold Article 4 and the bans in Article 5, have applied since 2 February 2025 [1, Art. 113(a)]. The eight bans include harmful manipulation, social scoring and AI that infers emotions at work, except for medical or safety reasons [10; 13, Art. 5(1)(f); 14]. In its 2024 wording, Article 4 required providers and deployers to take measures to ensure, "to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy" of their staff [1, Art. 4].

3.2 European Union, 2025: duties for general-purpose AI models begin

Chapter V, on providers of general-purpose AI models, has applied since 2 August 2025, but Article 101 on fines was left out of that date [1, Art. 113(b)], and the Commission's enforcement powers apply from 2 August 2026 [15]. Models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 have until 2 August 2027 [7]. The Commission and the AI Board call the code of practice published on 10 July 2025 "an adequate voluntary tool" [16]. On 14 October 2026, Executive Vice-President Virkkunen said the AI Office had sent requests for information on issues such as "security, external evaluation and monitoring how models are used" [6].

3.3 European Union, 2026: the AI Omnibus moves the high-risk dates

The Commission's proposal of 19 November 2025 would have started the high-risk rules six or twelve months after it found support measures ready, by 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028 at the latest [17, Art. 1(31)]. The final act was published in the Official Journal on 24 July and entered into force on 27 July 2026 [3, 4].

The adopted text turns those limits into fixed dates: 2 December 2027 for the high-risk uses listed in Annex III, and 2 August 2028 for AI in products covered by Annex I, in place of 2 August 2026 and 2 August 2027 [3, Art. 1(40); 1, Art. 113]. Recital 40 gives two reasons: standards and guidance came late, and so did national authorities [3, recital 40]. The new Article 4 requires "measures to support the development of AI literacy" but no "specific level" of it [3, Art. 1(5)]. Two new bans, one of them on AI that makes or alters realistic intimate or sexual images, videos or audio of an identifiable person without consent, apply from 2 December 2026 [3, Art. 1(7) and 1(40)].

3.4 Brussels, 2026: transparency duties start, with one grace period

Article 50 has applied since 2 August 2026 [2; 9, para 153]. Providers must design interactive AI such as chatbots so that people know they are dealing with AI, unless this is obvious. Providers of generative AI must mark its output in a machine-readable way. Deployers must disclose deepfakes and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest [18]. Providers of generative systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 for marking. The proposal had said 2 February 2027 [3, Art. 1(39); 17, Art. 1(30)]. Non-binding Commission guidelines of 20 July 2026 add that the grace period covers marking only, and that content made before 2 August 2026 need not be marked or labelled, except AI texts published from that date on [9, paras 5, 153, 154].

4. How practitioners handle it now

Each date is read from the article that sets it. The Commission's guidelines date Article 50 by citing Article 113 [9, para 153]. On 16 November 2026, the AI Act Service Desk's questions on the Omnibus still gave the proposal's "transition period of 6 months" [11]. The Commission's AI literacy page dated the Article 4 changes to "mid-July 2026" [12], and the EUR-Lex record of the act gives 27 July [3].

Each AI system is listed with its role and type. The AI Pact's voluntary pledges include "identifying and mapping AI systems likely to be categorised as high-risk under the AI Act" [8], and Article 50 gives providers and deployers different duties [18].

The date each system reached the market is kept on file. Article 111 gives older general-purpose AI models and generative systems more time. Older high-risk systems are covered only after a significant change in design, and those meant for public authorities must comply by 2 August 2030 [7]. Recital 39 of the Omnibus counts from the first unit of each high-risk type and model on the EU market [3, recital 39].

AI literacy work is recorded, without a test or certificate. The Commission says "There is no need for a certificate", that organisations "can keep an internal record of trainings", and that Article 4 does not require measuring employees' knowledge. National market surveillance authorities supervise the duty from 2 August 2026 [12].

A person fact-checks AI-written public text before it goes out unlabelled. The Commission's guidelines say "Fact-checking the accuracy of the content is a minimum requirement". Spell-checks and "cursory editorial approval" do not count, and whoever holds editorial responsibility should be named publicly, with contact details. One accepted example is a listed company's AI-generated sustainability report on its website, reviewed by professionals in a function such as compliance [9, section 6.2.3].

5. What to take from this

For people who use AI at work. An AI-written text published to inform the public on matters of public interest needs a label unless it was reviewed, with a fact-check, under someone's editorial responsibility [9, 18]. AI that infers emotions at work has been banned since February 2025, except for medical or safety reasons [13].

For managers. A list of AI systems with each one's role, type and market date shows the date for each [2, 7]. The next three dates are 2 December 2026, 2 August 2027 and 2 December 2027 [5, 7].

For compliance teams and training providers. Article 4 now asks for measures that "support the development of AI literacy", with no required level [3]. Training material written before July 2026 that asks for "a sufficient level" describes the old text [1, Art. 4].

6. Limits of this report

National penalty rules, which each Member State had to adopt, were not checked [5]. Commission guidelines are non-binding, and authoritative interpretation belongs to the Court of Justice [9, 14]. Other Omnibus changes are not covered [3]. Article 5 is kept under review, and the Omnibus has already added to it, so duties and dates may change again [3, recital 10]. This report is not legal advice.
