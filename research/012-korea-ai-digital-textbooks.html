Korea's AI digital textbooks

How a national rollout planned for 2025 became optional, and what a state audit and teacher surveys found

IAPQB Report No. 12 · Case file · Published 17 November 2026
International AI Practice Qualification Board (IAPQB)

In one minute

  • In March 2025, South Korea brought AI digital textbooks for English, maths and informatics into some school grades. Schools could choose, and 3,870 of 11,932 schools (32.4%) chose them for at least one subject [1, 2, 3].
  • On 4 August 2025, parliament voted to make them optional educational materials [4, 5]. Concerns included screen time, literacy and budgets, and 79.7% of teachers using them reported more work [4, 6, 7].
  • A state audit found that from March to May 2025, on average 60% of students in schools that chose the books never logged in, and that the Ministry of Education had skipped the pilot [8, 9, 10].
  • Practices urged or adopted include pilots and effect checks, counts of actual use, consultation and budget forecasts, data-protection checks and, in Sweden, a state textbook grant [10, 11, 12, 13].

1. A familiar situation

In February 2025, a primary head teacher in Korea has a few weeks to decide whether to use AI textbooks for third- and fourth-grade English and maths. Subject teachers and the school committee must review the choice first [14]. Some teachers want to try the books. Others ask who will pay next year, and whether the books help children learn.

2. Why take-up stayed low

From 2025, schools chose whether to sign up and teachers whether to use the books, weighing what they added against the time they took. In the audit's survey, teachers who did not use the books most often said they were little more than digitised paper textbooks [8, 9], and in a teachers' federation survey 79.7% of those using them reported more work [6, 7]. The Ministry had also put subscription costs on regional offices without proper consultation [8, 10].

The Global Education Monitoring (GEM) Report team wrote in 2023 that impartial evidence of technology's added value in learning "is needed more than ever, but is lacking", and asked whether a technology is appropriate, equitable, scalable and sustainable [15].

IAPQB view. Here the start date came first. The Ministry fixed 2025 after seven internal meetings, skipped the pilot and issued the certification notice before setting technical standards [8, 9, 10]. A third of schools signed up, and in those schools, on average, 60% of students never logged in [1, 8, 10].

3. Four cases

3.1 South Korea, 2025: a third of schools sign up

In June 2023, the Ministry of Education announced AI digital textbooks for 2025: maths, English and informatics in the third and fourth grades of primary school and the first year of middle and high school [1, 16]. On 31 January 2025, it made their use a choice for each school [2]. Ministry figures reported in March showed that 3,870 of 11,932 schools (32.4%) had chosen them for at least one subject, from nearly every school in Daegu to 8% in Sejong [1, 3, 14].

3.2 Seoul, 2025: parliament removes the textbook status

The opposition-led National Assembly first voted on 26 December 2024 to limit textbooks to printed books and e-books [17]. Acting President Choi Sang-mok's Cabinet vetoed it on 21 January 2025 [5, 18]. After a change of government, the Assembly passed an amendment to the Elementary and Secondary Education Act on 4 August 2025, with effect on promulgation, making AI textbooks optional educational materials [4, 5]. Yonhap reported concerns that they could "excessively increase screen time", harm literacy and strain budgets. Opposition People Power Party lawmakers denounced the amendment as a setback for classroom innovation [4].

A week earlier, the Korean Federation of Teachers' Associations had published a survey of 3,485 teachers: 80.4% viewed the books negatively, 87.4% said preparation and support were lacking and 78.9% preferred the status of educational material [6, 7].

3.3 Seoul, 2025: the state audit

At the National Assembly's request, the Board of Audit and Inspection (BAI) audited the rollout and reported in December 2025 [8, 10]. In schools that chose the books, from March to May 2025, an average of 8.1% of students used them on ten or more days and 60% never logged in [8, 9, 10, 19]. The Ministry had set the 2025 start after seven internal meetings, without consulting students, parents or teachers. It skipped the pilot for lack of time, issued the certification notice before setting technical standards, and told regional education offices to pay subscription fees from their general grants without proper consultation. The BAI issued a caution to the Ministry [8, 9, 10].

3.4 Seoul, 2026: publishers go to court

Figures from two lawmakers put school sign-ups for the second semester of 2025 at about half or less of the first-semester number [20, 21]. Two publishers had sued the Ministry in April 2025 over its switch to school choice. On 23 July 2026, the Seoul Administrative Court dismissed the case without ruling on the merits, finding the Ministry's guidance was not a decision open to such a suit [3, 5, 22, 23]. In June 2026, about ten publishers sued the government for damages, and publishers have also filed a constitutional complaint against the August 2025 amendment [19, 23, 24, 25].

4. How practitioners handle it now

Pilot first, and check effects before scaling. The BAI asked the Ministry to pilot new kinds of textbooks, check their effects and set technical standards before introducing them [9, 10]. The GEM Report team wrote that continuing Korea's programme should rest on "strong evidence" that it improves children's learning [11].

Measure use as well as sign-ups. The audit's login counts showed far less use than the 32.4% school take-up suggested [1, 8, 10].

Consult teachers, parents and funders before fixing a date. The BAI faulted the Ministry for not hearing students, parents or teachers, and for placing costs on regional offices without proper consultation [8, 9, 10]. The GEM Report team wrote that training, "budgetary forecasts" and communication with parents are not easy for school leaders, who need clear rules [11].

Prepare teachers, and ask them whether it worked. Korea planned 1,200 digital tutors to help schools, and USD 760 million over three years to train teachers [2, 11]. In the federation's survey, 61.0% of those trained found it not useful [6, 7].

Check data protection before a school picks a tool. Under standards announced on 29 December 2025, Korean schools choosing learning software, including the former AI textbooks, must check it against criteria set with the Personal Information Protection Commission, such as minimal collection of personal data. The school committee reviews the choice and the head teacher decides [12, 26]. The teachers' federation said the checks would add to teachers' work [26].

Fund books, and bring in screens by age. Sweden introduced a state grant for textbooks and teacher guides in 2023, setting aside SEK 685 million that year, and aims at one textbook per pupil per subject. Its government says physical books matter for learning and digital tools should come in only at an age when they support it. Since 1 July 2025, the preschool curriculum no longer requires them [13].

5. What to take from this

For teachers and school leaders. Before signing up for an AI tool, a school can ask whether it was piloted and what the pilot showed [10]. In the first term, the audit's two counts show whether it is used [8].

For ministries and education offices. In Korea, a start date set without consultation and before technical standards, and kept by skipping the pilot, was followed by an audit caution, a change in the law and lawsuits [4, 10, 22]. Each step can be dated before a launch is announced.

For companies that build AI learning products. Korean publishers built products as textbooks that lost that status after one semester, and some now seek damages [24, 25]. Under the December 2025 standards, schools may choose a product only if it meets data-protection criteria [12, 26].

6. Limits of this report

Most sources are Korean-language news reports. Apart from the school counts below, each news figure appeared in two outlets or a wire report. The BAI report did not load, so the audit findings come from four news reports [8, 9, 10, 19]. School counts differ between data sets: 3,870 in March 2025, and 4,095 or 4,146 in later first-semester counts, each from one outlet [1, 20, 21]. A 2026 count could not be confirmed in two sources, nor whether the December 2025 standards have changed. The survey came from a teachers' organisation with views on the policy, and the Swedish measures cover digital tools generally. No study of the books' effect on learning was found, and no court had ruled on the merits in the sources cited, the newest dated July 2026.
